Method notes in brief

The short version: what the tool computes, which published method each quantity implements, and — the part that matters most when you cite it — what each number actually describes. It contains no building model at all, deliberately. The full method notes give the source values and the references behind every statement here.

Three things to know before you cite a number

  1. The comfort figures describe outdoor air, and there are two of them. The Comfortable hours tile is the share of outdoor hours a person standing outside in still air would find comfortable — no building, fabric, gain, system or control enters it. A second tile, Outside comfort band, is a different quantity computed a different way: dry bulb alone against two sliders you set, with humidity ignored. Say which one you are quoting.
  2. There is no adaptive comfort in this tool, although the code contains it. The ASHRAE 55 adaptive band is implemented and tested, and no view calls it — no control reaches it and no chart draws it. Every comfort figure reported is the static, still-air zone. For a naturally ventilated building in a warm climate that understates comfort, and the tool cannot be cited for adaptive comfort at all.
  3. A wind frequency means nothing without its binning. The rose offers 8, 12, 16 or 36 sectors and fixed speed bands, with hours below 0.5 m/s held out as calm. Change the sector count and every percentage changes, because the same hours divide among a different number of bins. A quoted frequency must name the tool, the sector count and the speed bands.

What it calculates

Seven families of quantity. Two — solar position and the psychrometric state — are computed for every hour of the file the moment it loads. The rest are reductions over whatever hours the analysis period selects: comfortable hours against the ASHRAE 55 still-air zones, three Givoni–Milne passive strategies, degree days, descriptive statistics, and the wind rose.

The arithmetic here is not a rule of thumb. It is an implementation of somebody's published method — ASHRAE Handbook formulations for the psychrometrics, NOAA for the solar geometry — which raises the standard for provenance and for the scope of any claim made from it.

Not modelled at all

The big one
Any building
No fabric, no U-value, no thermal mass, no glazing, no zone, no occupancy, no internal gain, no system and no control. The massing block casts a shadow and has no other property: it is a solid with dimensions, not a building.
Out
The other four comfort variables
Radiant temperature, air speed, clothing and metabolic rate enter nothing. The comfort evaluation is a rectangle on the psychrometric plane, fixed at still air. No PMV, no PPD, no SET.
Out
Any energy calculation
No load, no demand, no consumption, no plant, no carbon. Degree days are a climate statistic here, not an energy estimate — multiplying them by anything to get a load is your assumption, not the tool's.
Out
Radiation on anything but the horizontal
The file's global, direct and diffuse values are read and plotted as recorded. Nothing is transposed onto a tilted or vertical surface, so there is no irradiation on a façade and no incident-angle calculation.
Out
Trend, projection, and design conditions
A typical meteorological year is synthesised from months of different real years: no date in it is a date that happened, so there is no trend to extract. And the DESIGN CONDITIONS header an engineer sizes plant against is in every EPW file and never read.
Partly
Daylight, and local clock time
The file's illuminance columns are plotted and reduced like any other variable but nothing is computed from them — this is daylight availability, not daylight modelling. Everything is local standard time; daylight saving is not read, so a summer occupied-hours analysis is an hour out from the clock occupants keep.

What it is safe to cite this tool for. Cite it as an instrument for reading a weather file: the climate's structure through the year, its psychrometric shape, its solar geometry, its wind regime, and which passive strategies its outdoor conditions sit near. Do not cite it for a building's performance, for a comfort result about occupants, for an energy or carbon figure, or for anything that must satisfy a regulation. It computes properties of a climate, and a climate is not a building.

How exact the numbers are

Because these quantities are defined by published standards, “accuracy” splits into three questions that are easy to run together and should not be. The third dominates.

Checked
Fidelity to the standard
Where the source cites an equation number it implements that equation with its published coefficients — saturation pressure agrees with ASHRAE Fundamentals to within 0.05% across −10 to 40 °C. A handful of simplifications are deliberate and recorded: three Givoni polygons rather than the full set, strategy percentages counted independently so they overlap and sum to more than 100, and degree-day totals never unit-converted.
Sufficient
Numerical precision
Every hourly series is stored as a 32-bit float, so about seven significant digits survive. For weather data this is the right trade and costs nothing that matters: the measurements themselves carry two or three significant figures.
Dominates
The input
A typical meteorological year is one synthesised year for one station. Its months come from different real years, so it has no interannual variability and contains no real extreme event in its real sequence — and it describes the station, not your site. A rooftop three kilometres away and forty metres higher, inside an urban heat island, is a different climate. That distance is larger than every numerical consideration above, combined.

What is asserted, and what is not. An automated suite of 53 tests making 229 assertions runs against three real weather files, covering the psychrometrics, the solar geometry, the statistics, period selection and parsing. That tells you the tool computes what it says it computes. It says nothing about whether the weather file is a good description of your site — and no view module has a unit test: not one of the eight 2D charts, not one of the four 3D scenes. The physics is well asserted; the drawing of it is asserted by eye.

Three exercises

Each isolates one idea and holds everything else fixed. The app ships with no weather data, so begin by downloading one — climate.onebuilding.org and energyplus.net/weather both publish free EPW files by station. Record the station name and the source for every file you use.

Exercise 01

A heating season is a shape, and a monthly mean hides it

  1. Load a temperate-climate file. Thermal mode, Annual heatmap, variable Dry bulb temperature, period 1 Jan–31 Dec.
  2. Read the picture before any number. Where does the cold block sit, and at which hours of the day does it reach furthest into the year? Write down the two dates where the cold reaches the middle of the day.
  3. Switch to Monthly statistics. Write down the mean for the coldest month, then the 5th and 95th percentile whiskers for that same month.
  4. Back to the heatmap. Set the degree-day base to 18, then 15.5, then 12, watching the colour boundary move, and record the heating degree-day tile at each base.

Principle. A monthly mean is one number standing for about 720 hours, and it averages away exactly what a designer needs: how cold, for how long, and at what time of day. The whiskers begin to restore that; the heatmap restores it completely, because the eye reads a two-dimensional field faster than any table. Degree days ask a third way, re-weighting by how far below the base an hour sat. The base is your assumption about the building, not a property of the climate — which is why a degree-day figure quoted without its base is unusable.

Exercise 02

A climate rewards one passive strategy and refuses another

  1. Comfort mode, Psychrometric chart, passive strategies on, period 1 Jan–31 Dec.
  2. Look at where the cloud of hours sits relative to the comfort rectangles — left, inside, above, or to the right. Do this before reading any percentage.
  3. Write down the percentage against each of the three strategies. Note that they overlap, and that they do not sum to 100.
  4. Change the period to 1 Jun–31 Aug and re-read all three; then to the occupied preset, 08–18, and re-read again.
  5. Read the Comfortable hours tile, then the Outside comfort band tile. Write down both, and one sentence on why they disagree.

Principle. The position of the cloud decides the strategy before any arithmetic does. A cloud sitting left of comfort is a heating climate, and no amount of ventilation moves it rightwards. A cloud high and to the right is warm and humid, where evaporative cooling has nothing to work with. The same climate recommends a different strategy over summer than over the year, and a different one again over occupied hours — which is why a strategy claim that does not carry its period is not a claim about anything.

Exercise 03

Latitude fixes the sun and almost nothing else

  1. Download two files at nearly the same latitude but in different situations — one maritime and western-coastal, one deep inland and continental. Aim for within two degrees.
  2. Load the first. Solar mode, Sun path dome. Note the solstice arcs and the day length. Load the second into the same view and satisfy yourself that the sun path is almost identical. That is the control in this experiment.
  3. Turn on Compare, source Two files, second file into panel B. Go to Annual heatmap, then Distribution, then Monthly statistics.
  4. Record for both: annual mean, annual minimum and maximum, and the difference between the warmest and coldest monthly mean. Then read both psychrometric charts.

Principle. Two sites at the same latitude receive the same sun on the same days, and can still differ by twenty kelvin in annual range. Ocean, altitude, prevailing wind and distance from a coast do the rest, and they do far more of it than latitude. This is the exercise that stops “what latitude is it?” being the first question asked about a site. One caution specific to this tool: in two-file compare the colour scales are computed separately for each panel, so the same colour means a different temperature on the left and on the right. Read the numbers, not the colours.

A caution for all three: quote the settings with the figure. Every number these exercises produce depends on a file you chose, a period you set and, for the comfort figures, on band limits and a humidity limit. A percentage without them cannot be checked by anyone reading you.

Where it stops

The tool is built to make a climate legible — its structure through the year, its psychrometric shape, its solar geometry, its wind regime. For anything that must stand up, use a simulation tool and a measured or morphed weather file appropriate to the site. It contains no building model at all, deliberately, so that what it does do stays honest — and the weather file's distance from your actual site is larger than every numerical consideration in it.

References

Two rules govern this list. It contains only what the shipped application itself names, in its own words. Where the code names nothing, nothing is listed and the absence is recorded instead. The code names a chapter and four equation numbers for the psychrometrics and nothing else — no edition, no year, no clause anywhere — so every edition and year below is this document's choice, not a transcription.

Named in the application

ASHRAE. (2021). ASHRAE handbook — fundamentals (SI ed.). — the only source cited by equation number: atmospheric pressure at altitude, saturation vapour pressure over ice and over water, and the adiabatic-saturation relation the wet-bulb bisection solves. The coefficients have been unchanged since 2009, so the edition does not affect the values.

ASHRAE. (2023). Thermal environmental conditions for human occupancy (ANSI/ASHRAE Standard 55-2023). — the still-air comfort zones, the adaptive band and the prevailing mean outdoor temperature. Only the first is reachable in the application. One caution: the code attributes α = 0.8 to Standard 55, but the Standard allows 0.6 to 0.9 and fixes no value — 0.8 is the EN 16798-1 default. Do not cite Standard 55 as the authority for 0.8 itself.

Implemented without attribution

Eight things bear on a number a reader might quote and the code names no source for any of them: the degree-day convention (hourly then divided by 24 — not the daily-mean convention of most published series, and the code does not say so), the percentile interpolation (the spreadsheet convention; others give different answers), the humidity-ratio and enthalpy constants, the circular mean, the point-in-polygon rule that decides which hours count as comfortable, the shadow projection, the “standard” 90.833° zenith that sets every sunrise time, and the OKLab colour ramps. This is the honest counterpart to the list above, and the reason the page does not claim more provenance than it has.
